Pauling's peace plea gets a standing ovation

Nobelist Linus Pauling called on the American people Wednesday night "to bring this (war) madness to an end."

The response to this plea and his emotional speech drew a one-minute standing ovation from the Vietnam moratorium supporters who jammed into Memorial Auditorium and Memorial Church at Stanford University.

Pauling was with the University of California, San Diego, until he joined the Stanford faculty as a chemistry professor this year.

Pauling won the Nobel Prize for chemsitry in 1954 and the Nobel Prize for peace in 1962.

He charged that "the American people are now learning the truth about the war: Its cold-blooded planning by John Foster Dulles to keep part of Southeast Asia from leaving our economic sphere; our refusal in 1954 to sign the Geneva accord which called for a democratic election under international supervision to permit the people of Vietnam to choose the government they preferred, and our entry into the war on a great scale without even a request from South Vietnam.

"We are face to face in opposition to the small group of rich and powerful people who are using their power to keep the war going," he continued.

"They are the poeple who will benefit from the war, the military - industrial complex, the Pentagon and the war contractors who get the $15 billion per year of excess profits on the guns, bombs, napalm, planes and other instruments of war, and also the politicians, such as President Nixon, who are indebted to them."

He reminded his listeners that four years earlier he had joined seven other Nobel Peace Prize winners in an appeal to the governments involved to stop the war in Vietnam.

"President Johnson did not answer this appeal," Pauling said. "Ho Chi Minh did, saying that he hoped for a peace based on the principles of the Geneva accord."
